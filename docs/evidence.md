# Memory Lane: evidence for the pitch

Every fact below was checked against the source in November 2026. Organisation pages (WHO, ADI, Alzheimer Europe, NICE, NHS, Alzheimer's Society, Cochrane) were opened directly; journal abstracts were confirmed through their Europe PMC records because the publisher pages block automated access, so the DOI links are given for readers. The NICE website also blocked automated access, so the NG97 wording was confirmed from the official NICE guideline PDF as hosted by NHS Scotland (https://rightdecisions.scot.nhs.uk/media/1842/nice-guidance-dementia-assessment-management-and-support-for-people-living-with-dementia-and-their-carers.pdf). Anything that could not be confirmed is left out and listed at the end.

## 1. How many people live with dementia

- 57 million people were living with dementia worldwide in 2021, with nearly 10 million new cases every year (WHO dementia fact sheet, 2021 figure) — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia
- The number is projected to reach 78 million by 2030 and 139 million by 2050 (WHO, 2021; the same projection is used by Alzheimer's Disease International) — https://www.who.int/news/item/02-09-2021-world-failing-to-address-dementia-challenge and https://www.alzint.org/about/dementia-facts-figures/dementia-statistics/
- An estimated 9,065,706 people live with dementia in the EU27 in 2025, rising to 14,335,788 by 2050 (a 58% increase); for the EU27 plus twelve non-EU European countries the figures are 12,122,979 and 19,905,856 (a 64% increase). Finland: 117,556 people in 2025 (2.09% of the population), rising to 169,337 in 2050 (3.16%) (Alzheimer Europe, *The Prevalence of Dementia in Europe 2025*, published January 2026; Finland is section 6.11) — https://www.alzheimer-europe.org/dementia/prevalence-dementia-europe (report PDF: https://www.alzheimer-europe.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/alzheimer_europe_-_dementia_prevalence_report_2025_-_final_-_digital.pdf)

Note: the Alzheimer Europe figures are modelled estimates (prevalence rates applied to UN population projections), not counts of diagnosed people. The Finnish Academy of Science and Letters gives a separate figure of about 150,000 *diagnosed* people with a memory disorder in Finland, about 23,000 new diagnoses a year, and says the number "may even double by 2050" (press release, 27 May 2025) — https://www.sttinfo.fi/tiedote/71192284/muistisairaiden-maara-kaksinkertaistuu-suomessa-tutkijoiden-mukaan-nopeasti-yleistyva-kansansairaus-haastaa-sote-palvelut-ennennakemattomalla-tavalla?lang=fi. The two sources define the group differently, so do not mix them in one sentence.

## 2. Repetitive questioning

- In a study of 1,707 people with dementia whose symptoms were tracked through an online tool, verbal repetition was chosen as a treatment target for 807 of them (47.3%), and repetitive questioning was the most common form. It was tracked most often in mild dementia and in Alzheimer's disease. Caveat: a self-selected online sample, and "chosen as a target" measures what users most wanted to change, not how common the symptom is overall (Reeve, Molin, Hui and Rockwood, *International Psychogeriatrics*, 2017) — https://doi.org/10.1017/S1041610216002180 (open copy: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5426314/)
- "Repetitive questioning is a major problem for caregivers"; a companion case study refers to "the angst that caregivers experience with repetitive questioning". Caveat: these are clinical case-study papers expressing expert opinion, not measurements of burden (Hamdy et al., *Gerontology & Geriatric Medicine*, 2018) — https://doi.org/10.1177/2333721417740190 and https://doi.org/10.1177/2333721417738915
- Alzheimer's Society explains that the person may not remember the answer they were given, is often unaware they are repeating themselves, and that "often if someone is repeating the same question, they need an emotional rather than factual response" (Alzheimer's Society UK, page opened November 2026) — https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/about-dementia/symptoms-and-diagnosis/symptoms/repetitive-behaviour

## 3. Carer burden

- Informal carers (most often family members and friends) provide on average 5 hours of care and supervision a day; women provide 70% of care hours; informal care accounts for about half of the global cost of dementia, which was US$ 1.3 trillion in 2019 (WHO fact sheet and WHO news release on the Global status report, 2021; 2019 data) — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dementia and https://www.who.int/news/item/02-09-2021-world-failing-to-address-dementia-challenge
- About 82 billion hours of informal care were given to people with dementia living at home in 2015, equal to 2,089 hours a year or 6 hours a day per person (Alzheimer's Disease International, *Global estimates of informal care*, Wimo, Gauthier and Prince, 2018) — https://www.alzint.org/resource/global-estimates-of-informal-care/
- A meta-analysis of 43 studies (16,911 informal carers of people with dementia) found a pooled prevalence of depression of 31.24% (95% CI 27.70 to 35.01) and of burden of 49.26% (95% CI 37.15 to 61.46). Caveat: results varied widely between studies and by the questionnaire used (Collins and Kishita, *Ageing & Society*, 2020) — https://doi.org/10.1017/S0144686X19000527 (repository record: https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/70555/)

## 4. Reminiscence therapy and life-story work

- The Cochrane review covered 22 trials with 1,972 people with dementia. Its conclusion: "The effects of reminiscence interventions are inconsistent, often small in size" and differ considerably by setting and format. There was no clear overall effect on quality of life (SMD 0.11, 95% CI -0.12 to 0.33), a probable slight benefit in care homes, a very small gain in cognition right after treatment that did not last, a probable small benefit to communication from group and community reminiscence, and a probable slight improvement in mood from individual reminiscence. No effect on carers was found, apart from a possible increase in carer anxiety in two studies of joint reminiscence (Woods et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2018, CD001120) — https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD001120_reminiscence-therapy-dementia (full review: https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001120.pub3)
- NICE recommends: "Offer a range of activities to promote wellbeing that are tailored to the person's preferences" (1.4.1) and "Consider group reminiscence therapy for people living with mild to moderate dementia" (1.4.3). "Consider" is NICE's weaker wording, used where the evidence of benefit is less certain (NICE guideline NG97, 2018) — https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations

## 5. Recorded family voices and personalised messages

- The Cochrane review of simulated presence therapy (playing audio or video recordings made by family members) found only three trials with 144 participants, all in nursing homes. Results were mixed and depended on how behaviour was measured, the evidence was rated very low quality, and no trial measured quality of life or effects on carers. The authors wrote: "We were unable to draw any conclusions about the efficacy of SPT" (Abraha et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2020 update, CD011882) — https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD011882_simulated-presence-therapy-dementia (full review: https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD011882.pub3)
- A pilot cross-over trial of a "Memory Box" device holding family-made messages, photographs and music, completed by 11 nursing home residents with dementia and daily agitation, found the device was well used and highly rated by residents, families and staff, and that depressive and anxiety symptoms fell during use; the abstract does not report a significant fall in agitation. Caveat: 11 people, a pilot, single-blinded (Davison et al., *Geriatric Nursing*, 2016) — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gerinurse.2015.08.013

## 6. Guidance on answering repeated questions

- Alzheimer's Society: "Try to be patient and keep your tone level and calm", think about what need might lie behind the behaviour, and consider distraction or an activity the person enjoys. It advises an emotional rather than a purely factual response (Alzheimer's Society UK, page opened November 2026) — https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/about-dementia/symptoms-and-diagnosis/symptoms/repetitive-behaviour
- NHS: the person "is not being deliberately difficult"; be tactful and patient, help them find the answer themselves (for example an easy-to-read clock for repeated questions about the time), look for an underlying theme such as feeling lost, and offer reassurance (NHS, "Coping with dementia behaviour changes", page opened November 2026) — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/dementia/living-with-dementia/behaviour/
- NICE says carers should be offered training that includes "how to understand and respond to changes in behaviour" and help "to adapt their communication styles" (1.11.1), and that psychosocial and environmental interventions should be the initial and ongoing way to reduce distress (1.7.2) (NICE guideline NG97, 2018) — https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations

Note: this guidance supports calm, patient, reassuring answers and helping the person find the answer themselves. None of it specifically recommends a recorded answer or a familiar voice; that part is our design idea, not established guidance. The guidance also stresses the feeling behind the question, which a fixed recorded answer may not meet.

## 7. Smart glasses and wearable cameras: what exists already

- SenseCam, a wearable camera from Microsoft Research: in six patients with Alzheimer's disease, reviewing SenseCam images of recent events led to more recalled detail over two weeks than a written diary in five of the six. Caveat: six people, and it is a camera for later review, not a real-time aid (Hodges, Watson, Kapur, Woodberry and Woodberry, journal *Memory*; Microsoft Research publication page, which dates it 2007 — check the journal's own year before citing) — https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=166065
- CrossSense, by Animorph Co-operative: smart-glasses software with an AI assistant that recognises everyday objects and gives audio and visual prompts linking objects to their names and uses, for people with early-stage dementia. It won the £1 million Longitude Prize on Dementia in 2026, and a University of Sussex study led by Professor Julia Simner has been testing it in participants' homes. No peer-reviewed results were found (Challenge Works; NIHR Join Dementia Research, 28 September 2025) — https://challengeworks.org/challenge-prizes/longitude-prize-on-dementia/ and https://joindementiaresearch.nihr.ac.uk/news/smart-glasses

What is new in Memory Lane, said carefully: existing work either records the wearer's own day for later review (SenseCam) or names and explains objects (CrossSense). Memory Lane attaches memories recorded by the family, in their own voices, to objects and people, and gives a family-recorded answer to a question the wearer keeps asking. We found no published study of that combination, which also means there is no evidence yet that it works.

## What we must not claim

- That Memory Lane treats, slows, prevents or reverses dementia, or improves memory or cognition. It is a support and comfort aid, and it is untested.
- That it is "clinically proven", "evidence-based" or "validated". We have run no study with people living with dementia. The honest wording is "informed by" reminiscence and carer-communication guidance.
- That reminiscence therapy "works". The Cochrane review found effects that are inconsistent and often small; NICE only says to "consider" it.
- That playing recorded family voices is proven to calm people or reduce agitation. Cochrane could draw no conclusion from three small trials, and reactions vary between people; a recording could also unsettle someone.
- That it reduces carer stress, burden or depression. We have not measured this, and the reminiscence review found no benefit to carers (and a possible rise in anxiety in joint reminiscence).
- That NICE, the NHS or Alzheimer's Society recommend recorded answers or endorse the product. Their guidance is about patient, calm, reassuring responses from people.
- That repeated questioning is "the most stressful" behaviour for carers. It is very common and widely described as wearing, but studies that rank distress tend to put aggression higher.
- That it replaces carers or human contact, or is safe for unsupervised use at every stage of dementia.
- That we are the first smart glasses for dementia. CrossSense exists and won the Longitude Prize on Dementia; our difference is family-recorded memories and answers.
- Statistics mixed across definitions or dates, such as the 2020 "over 55 million" (ADI) alongside the 2021 "57 million" (WHO), or Finland's 150,000 diagnosed alongside the 117,556 estimated. Pick one source per sentence.
- Sensitive content needs care too: an answer to "Where is my wife?" may involve a death. Do not suggest the product resolves the ethical question of what to say; the family and care professionals decide that.

## Not verified, so not used above

- Figures reported in the press for the CrossSense study (23 pairs; naming of household items 46% without the glasses, 82% with them, 78% an hour later; not peer-reviewed). The Guardian article could not be opened and no paper or preprint was found.
- "Repetitive questioning in 94.8% of people with Alzheimer's" from a 2025 US survey of 250 carers (Grossberg et al., *Dementia*). The figure appears in a trade-press report but not in the paper's abstract, and the full text was not opened.
- Cochrane Library, PubMed, PMC and nice.org.uk pages returned access blocks; the facts were confirmed from cochrane.org summaries, Europe PMC records and the NG97 PDF instead. The DOI links themselves were not opened.
